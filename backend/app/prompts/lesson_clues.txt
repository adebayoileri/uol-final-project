A clue is what a learner HEARS in a listening drill, before choosing which concept it describes. The audio is the question. If the clue contains the concept's name, the drill is worthless — it just announces the answer and tests nothing.

Write each clue so it can only be matched by understanding, never by word-spotting.

For each concept:

1. NEVER use the concept's own term. That includes:
   - any capitalisation or punctuation of it — "over-fitting" and "Over Fitting" are the same leak as "Overfitting";
   - plurals and other forms of it — "overfitted" and "overfitting" name the term just as plainly;
   - its acronym or abbreviation, if it has one;
   - a word that merely contains the term, such as "overfittingly";
   - THE KEY WORD OF A MULTI-WORD TERM. This is the rule most often broken. If the term is "Activation Function", you must not write "function"; if it is "Machine Learning", you must not write "learning" or "learn"; if it is "Confusion Matrix", you must not write "matrix". A clue that says "a mathematical function that..." has given away half the answer. Describe the job it does instead: "a rule that decides what a neuron outputs, given the total of its weighted inputs".
2. Do NOT name the other concepts in this lesson either. A clue that mentions a second term is ambiguous, and the learner sees both in the options.
3. Describe the mechanism, the situation it applies to, and what happens as a result — the same way you would explain it to someone who does not yet know the word for it. Say what it does, not what it is called.
4. Make exactly one of the options fit. If your clue would also describe a neighbouring concept, add the distinguishing detail that rules the others out.
5. Write it to be heard, not read:
   - one or two sentences, roughly 15 to 40 words;
   - plain spoken prose, as a person would say it aloud;
   - no markdown, no bullet characters, no code, no parentheses;
   - no symbols or notation — write "greater than zero point five", never "> 0.5";
   - no abbreviations that are read badly aloud;
   - no reference to "this lesson", "as we covered", or "the previous section" — the listener has only your sentence.
6. Do not phrase it as a question. The drill asks "Which concept was described?" after the clip; a question inside the clue competes with that.
7. Do not mention the options, the quiz, or the fact that this is a clue.

The clue should be specific enough that a learner who knows the material can answer confidently, and vivid enough that one who does not learns something from hearing it.
